Lesson 0005

The convoy

Why a one-second ALTER TABLE takes the site down instead of delaying one statement — and what to do when the most important operational fact in your database has no sentence in your database’s manual.

This is the shortest lesson in the topic and the one most worth keeping. It contains one mechanism and one sourcing problem, and the sourcing problem is not an aside — it is half the lesson.

The mechanism

Three actors. Work through them in order.

How a fast migration produces a slow outage.
Who Does what Result
A long-running transaction Read from the table an hour ago and never committed. Holds ACCESS SHARE Harmless on its own
Your migration Requests ACCESS EXCLUSIVE, which conflicts with ACCESS SHARE Waits. Indefinitely, by default
Every subsequent SELECT Requests ACCESS SHARE — which does not conflict with ACCESS SHARE Waits anyway
The sentence to keep

A waiting ACCESS EXCLUSIVE request blocks everything queued behind it, including requests that do not conflict with the lock already held.

That is why a migration which would have taken one second takes the site down. The migration is not slow. The migration is queued, and it is holding the door.

Notice what makes this counter-intuitive. Lesson 3 taught you that ACCESS SHARE conflicts with ACCESS EXCLUSIVE only — so reader-against-reader should never wait. And they do not wait for each other. They wait for the ALTER TABLE that is itself waiting, sitting between them in the queue. The conflict table describes lock holders; the queue adds a rule the conflict table does not contain.

Now the problem: PostgreSQL never states this

This is the single most consequential operational fact about DDL on PostgreSQL, and the PostgreSQL manual does not contain it. Not on the locking page, not on ALTER TABLE, not in the MVCC chapter. There is no sentence to quote.

Look at what session 2 asked for

Session 2 specified LOCK=NONE — an assertion that would have failed loudly if concurrent DML could not be permitted — and still produced the outage. LOCK=NONE governs the execution phase, not the brief exclusive metadata lock taken at commit.

“Online” does not mean “lock-free”. It means the long part is online. The short part still stops the world, and the short part is enough.

How to cite this, and how not to

Teaching the convoy from MySQL’s page while noting that PostgreSQL behaves the same way is defensible. It is an argument by analogy between two engines that both use a queued table-level lock, and it is labelled as one.

Citing PostgreSQL for it is not defensible, and you will see it done constantly — usually with a link to the explicit-locking page, which does not say it. If you repeat this fact, repeat the provenance with it.

The mitigation, and its wrinkle

Since the danger is waiting rather than working, the fix is to refuse to wait:

Two operational notes the manual makes explicit, and both are easy to get wrong:

  • The per-attempt scoping means it does not bound the statement. A multi-subcommand ALTER TABLE touching several relations can wait up to N × lock_timeout in total.
  • It must be below statement_timeout to do anything: “if statement_timeout is nonzero, it is rather pointless to set lock_timeout to the same or larger value, since the statement timeout would always trigger first.”
SQL Written for this lesson 
-- The discipline: bound the wait, then retry rather than queue.
SET lock_timeout = '2s';

ALTER TABLE orders ADD COLUMN note text;   -- fails fast if it would queue

-- On failure: wait, and try again. Do not raise the timeout.
-- The failure is the feature -- it is the outage you did not have.

Check yourself

Why do plain SELECTs stall behind a waiting ALTER TABLE?

The ALTER TABLE holds nothing yet — that is the whole point. It is waiting, and requests arriving after it queue behind it even though they would not conflict with the lock currently held. The pg_locks page hints at this with “holding or waiting for”, and MySQL states the equivalent rule outright.

Check yourself

Which source may you legitimately cite for the convoy behaviour?

No PostgreSQL page states the rule; all three PostgreSQL answers are pages people cite for it wrongly. MySQL 8.0’s 17.12.2 states it directly and demonstrates it in three sessions — usable, provided you say out loud that you are arguing from another engine that queues table-level locks the same way.

The question this leaves open

If holding ACCESS EXCLUSIVE briefly is survivable and holding it through a scan is not, the obvious move is to get the scans out from under it. PostgreSQL documents exactly how, and it is the closest thing to a blessed recipe in the whole manual: lesson 6.

Read next, from the source

MySQL 8.0, 17.12.2 Online DDL Performance and Concurrency — yes, MySQL, even though you are running PostgreSQL. It is two screens, and it is the only first-party writing that explains this mechanism as a mechanism. Read the three-session example slowly and notice how ordinary each session looks in isolation.

Your agent is your teacher. Worth pushing on: ask it to find a PostgreSQL page that states the convoy rule. Watching a careful search come back empty is a much stronger way to internalise “this is undocumented” than being told — and if it produces a page, read the page rather than the summary, because that would be a real finding.

On spacing

This is the natural long pause in the topic. Lessons 1–5 are the model; 6–8 are what you do with it. Before moving on, try to tell the three-actor story out loud with no notes, and then state who documents it and who does not. Both halves.
