SQL Schema Migrations and How Alembic Implements Them

What the database actually does when a migration runs — which lock, which rewrite, what a failure leaves behind — and then how one tool implements all of it, including the three places its guarantees run out.

Seventeen lessons, built to be spaced over days rather than read in one sitting. Lessons 1–8 are the database and never mention a tool; 9–17 are Alembic 1.14.1, introduced from zero. The five reference documents at the bottom are the ones built to be returned to.

Part one — what the database does

  • 0001 DDL inside a transaction

    The property every safe migration rests on, established the only way the manual allows — negatively. Plus the first lesson in sourcing: what to do when the sentence you want does not exist.

  • 0002 What a half-failed migration leaves

    Nothing, an index that charges rent while doing no work, or a schema you repair by hand. Includes why MySQL’s atomic DDL is not transactional DDL, in MySQL’s own words.

  • 0003 Lock modes

    One sentence dissolves the subject: a lock mode is nothing but the set of modes it conflicts with. After this, every later lesson is arithmetic.

  • 0004 The lock and rewrite table

    Two independent costs — which lock, and how much work under it — and the three rows where everyone’s intuition is wrong. One cell is honestly marked undocumented.

  • 0005 The convoy

    Why a one-second ALTER TABLE takes the site down instead of delaying one statement — and what to do when the most important fact about your database has no sentence in your database’s manual.

  • 0006 NOT VALID and VALIDATE

    Splitting one long lock into a short lock plus a long weak one. The documented pattern, why it is sound rather than a trick, and the four-step SET NOT NULL recipe the manual never assembles.

  • 0007 Concurrent index builds

    The other way to move work out from under a strong lock, the one recipe PostgreSQL blesses outright, and the waiting clause that stalls a build silently and indefinitely.

  • 0008 Expand and contract

    The move when there is no safe form of the operation — and the first thing here with no vendor source at all, so also a lesson in teaching responsibly when the manuals are silent.

Part two — what the tool does

  • 0009 What Alembic is

    Orientation from zero: a revision file, a pointer, and a table with one column. Four ideas, and everything after is a consequence of them.

  • 0010 Where the version write sits

    One with block answers it, one boolean equality decides the transaction boundary, and the pairing is why a failed deploy is recoverable on PostgreSQL and not on MySQL.

  • 0011 Branches, merges and heads

    A merge revision is a tuple. That is the whole definition — after which branch labels, multiple heads and upgrade head reduce to set arithmetic.

  • 0012 What autogenerate cannot see

    Not a list of limitations — a proof. Nine functions are the entire detection surface, and three for loops show why a column rename can never be detected.

  • 0013 How op.add_column() becomes SQL

    Nine hops from your revision file to the wire. Alembic writes two strings; SQLAlchemy writes the rest — which tells you where to look when the emitted SQL surprises you.

  • 0014 Offline mode is a compiler

    --sql renders a migration; it does not run it. Pedantic until you see a data migration produce a plausible, wrong script and hand it to a DBA.

  • 0015 Batch mode

    Why SQLite’s ALTER TABLE is weak — SQLite’s own answer is better than “it just is” — and what the move-and-copy workaround silently drops on the way.

  • 0016 Concurrent deploys

    What does Alembic do when two instances migrate at once? Nothing. Establishing that properly — as an absence, with a control group — is the whole lesson.

  • 0017 Reading the evidence

    Sixteen lessons rested on four grades of evidence. This one makes the grading the object of study, because it is the part that survives PostgreSQL 17 and Alembic 1.20.

How to study this

Storage strength, not fluency

The goal is retention in a year, not comprehension today. That means leaving gaps between lessons, and — before opening the next one — trying to state the previous one’s argument out loud from memory. The recall attempt is the learning; re-reading only feels like it.

The natural long pauses are after lesson 5, which is the hinge of the first half, and after lesson 8, where the subject changes from a database to a tool.

If you only keep four things

Lesson 4’s three surprising rows; lesson 5’s three actors; lesson 6’s four steps; and lesson 16’s one sentence on what the compare-and-swap does and does not catch. Whatever you cannot reproduce a week later is what to re-read — and only that.
