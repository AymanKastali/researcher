Topic

Primary Keys and External Identifiers in PostgreSQL

What a key actually costs inside a B-tree leaf page, why the gap between a random key and a time-ordered one is mostly a special case in the split code, and how to tell a documented guarantee from a #define from somebody's arithmetic.

Fifteen lessons, built to be spaced over days rather than read in one sitting. The five reference documents at the bottom are the ones designed to be come back to. PostgreSQL 18 is the version taught throughout.

Lessons

  • 0001 Two jobs, one column

    Internal identity and external reference share no criterion. Codd's 1970 definitions, the “arbitrarily selected” clause that disarms every appeal to the relational model, and why a primary key in PostgreSQL is a designation rather than a structure.

  • 0002 Inside a leaf page

    The three grades of evidence, the constants the manual actually states, the three it does not, and a per-entry model validated to the byte against a real page the manual prints.

  • 0003 The rightmost page

    The hinge. Depth barely moves, and the real difference is a special case in nbtsplitloc.c that is not in the manual: ~90% density at the right edge against ~50% everywhere else.

  • 0004 There is no clustered index

    Three quotations, and most imported advice stops applying. Plus write amplification, carefully labelled as the inference it is.

  • 0005 Churn, and why a key must never change

    HOT's first condition is the strongest argument in the topic. Plus bottom-up deletion, deduplication, why TOAST never applies, and the commands that make lesson 2's arithmetic unnecessary.

  • 0006 What PostgreSQL can generate

    The feature ledger with release-note evidence, and the sentence every summary drops: uuidv7()'s monotonicity is ensured only within one backend.

  • 0007 Identity columns and the gap myth

    The baseline: narrowest key, best index behaviour, immutable if you spell it ALWAYS. Gaps are guaranteed and are not a security feature.

  • 0008 UUIDv4 and UUIDv7

    One bit-layout difference, and a trade that maps onto lesson 3. Also where the RFC makes its single quantitative claim, and where to apply the evidence grading to a standards document.

  • 0009 ULID, Snowflake, and TypeID

    Judging a specification by its governance as well as its bits: a dormant spec with no security section, an archived repo with a coordination problem, and a presentation layer sold as a format.

  • 0010 Natural keys and the issuing authority

    The only candidate whose failure is scheduled by somebody else. ISBN went 10→13 digits on a date it chose, and the synthesis here survives every other decision in the topic.

  • 0011 Guessability, and a trap between two specs

    RFC 9562 §8 in full — UUIDs MUST NOT be security capabilities — and the sharpest finding in the topic, which exists only when you read ULID and the RFC against each other.

  • 0012 Collisions, and generating away from the database

    A question both specifications deliberately refuse to answer, and the capability that is the honest reason to accept a 128-bit key.

  • 0013 The dual-identifier pattern

    Refusing to choose between the two jobs — with the bill printed: four costs, of which the discipline cost is permanent, plus why the covering-index optimisation usually does nothing.

  • 0014 The decision ladder

    A claim the topic relied on that turns out to be undocumented, what the decoupling buys at the API boundary, and four rungs plus one sentence that survives all of them.

  • 0015 Reading the evidence

    Everything the sources refused to say, and the four questions to ask of any claim. PostgreSQL 19 will change some answers above; it will not change how to check them.

How to study this

Storage strength, not fluency

The goal is retention in a year, not comprehension today. That means leaving gaps between lessons, and — before opening the next one — trying to state the previous one's argument out loud from memory. The recall attempt is the learning; re-reading only feels like it.

Lessons 1–5 are the physical mechanics, 6–10 the candidates, 11–12 security and distribution, 13–15 the decision and how to check it. The natural long pauses are after 5 and after 10. Lesson 3 is the one to over-learn: almost everything later leans on it.

A note on what is not here

There are no benchmark numbers anywhere in this topic, because no primary source publishes one. Every performance statement is either a quoted mechanism or arithmetic labelled as derived. Where you expect a figure and find none, that is deliberate — and Unverified claims records every such case.
