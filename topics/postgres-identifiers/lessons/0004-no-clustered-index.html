Lesson 0004

There is no clustered index

One sentence, three quotations, and most of the internet's advice on this subject stops applying. This is the lesson that lets you read a confident blog post and know immediately which engine its author learned on.

“Random primary keys fragment your table” is the most widely repeated claim in this area. It is true — in MySQL's InnoDB and in SQL Server. In PostgreSQL the mechanism it describes does not exist, and three documented facts establish that.

The three facts

What follows

In an engine with a clustered index, the primary key is the table's physical order. Inserting a row with a random key means inserting it into the middle of the table's own storage, so a random key scatters row insertions and fragments the table itself.

In PostgreSQL that mechanism does not exist. Heap inserts go to whatever page the free space map offers, regardless of key value. The index is a separate file that happens to point at them.

The conclusion

The cost of a random key in PostgreSQL is confined to the B-tree index on that key. It does not fragment your table. Any advice that says otherwise was written for a different engine.

Note the grade: the manual never states this conclusion in so many words. It follows from the three quotations above, and it is the only inference in this lesson that this topic is willing to make flatly — because each premise is a documented guarantee and the step between them is one of definition, not of mechanism.

Check yourself

What does CLUSTER actually do in PostgreSQL?

“Clustering is a one-time operation”, in the manual's own words, and afterwards “no attempt is made to store new or updated rows according to their index order”. The name is borrowed from engines where clustering is a maintained property; in PostgreSQL it names a one-shot rewrite. Nothing ever merges an index into the heap — all indexes are secondary, always.

Write amplification, carefully

There is a second cost often attributed to random keys, and it is real, but the evidence for it is weaker than for anything else in lessons 2 to 4. Being precise about that is the point of this section.

The documented mechanism:

That establishes something precise: the cost is per distinct page dirtied after a checkpoint, and each one costs about 8 kB of WAL. It is about pages touched, not rows written.

Now the inference, labelled as one

Random keys scatter inserts across many leaf pages (lesson 3) → more distinct pages are dirtied per checkpoint → more full-page images → more WAL.

No PostgreSQL document states this chain. The docs establish the full_page_writes mechanism and the rightmost-page insert behaviour in completely separate places and never connect them. It is a sound inference from two quoted facts, and this topic will not pretend it is more than that.

If it does bite you, wal_compression compresses full-page images specifically. Its default value is off.

Check yourself

What does full_page_writes cost per checkpoint interval?

The manual says “the first modification of that page after a checkpoint”, so the unit is the page, and only its first dirtying counts. That is exactly why insert locality could matter: twenty rows landing on one page cost one image, while twenty rows landing on twenty pages cost twenty. The connection to key ordering, though, is your inference and not the manual's.

How to read the internet after this lesson

Claims you will meet, and what this lesson does to them.
Claim Verdict in PostgreSQL
“Random keys fragment your table.” False here. No clustered index, so the heap is unaffected. Imported from InnoDB or SQL Server.
“Random keys bloat your index.” True, and lesson 3 gives the mechanism and roughly the magnitude.
“Random keys cost you a B-tree level.” Almost never. Lesson 3's depth table: zero extra levels below about ten billion rows.
“Random keys increase WAL volume.” Probably true, undocumented. A sound inference from two quoted facts that no PostgreSQL page connects.
“UUID inserts are an order of magnitude slower.” Unsourced. No primary source publishes a benchmark — lesson 15 traces where that number came from.

Where this is going

Lessons 2 to 4 have priced inserting a key. Lesson 5 prices changing one — which turns out to be the strongest argument in the whole topic, and the one with the least folklore around it. It also closes out the mechanics with the thing that makes all of this arithmetic unnecessary: measuring your own database instead.

Read next, from the source

PostgreSQL 18 manual, §11.9 “Index-Only Scans and Covering Indexes” — short, and it opens with the sentence this lesson turns on: all indexes are secondary. Read the whole page now rather than later, because its second half is the visibility-map dependency that lesson 13 needs.

Your agent is your teacher. A good use of it here: bring a blog post arguing against UUID keys and work through which of its claims survive this lesson. Most such posts contain a mix, and separating the mechanism from the imported assumption is the skill this topic is really building.

On spacing

Lessons 2, 3 and 4 are one argument in three parts, so this is a natural place to pause for longer. Before lesson 5, try to state the whole chain from memory: what an entry costs, what a split does, where the cost is confined, and which step of that chain is an inference rather than a documented fact.
