Lesson 0005

Churn, and why a key must never change

The usual argument for immutable keys is aesthetic — “an identifier shouldn't mean anything”. There is a mechanical one that is far stronger, and it is one documented paragraph long. This lesson closes out the mechanics with it, and with the tools that make all of lesson 2's arithmetic unnecessary.

So far the topic has priced writing a key once. Updating one is a different and much larger bill, and PostgreSQL prints it explicitly in the documentation for an optimisation you silently lose.

HOT: the mechanical argument

Read the first condition again

It says any columns referenced by the table's indexes — not “the index you are thinking about”. An indexed column that changes forfeits HOT for that row and forces a new index entry in every index on the table, including the ones that have nothing to do with the column you changed.

That is the argument. It is not about elegance, and it does not depend on anyone's taste in schema design. A mutable key is a standing tax on every update to the row, paid across every index the table carries.

“Copied into the referencing row(s)” means rewriting every child row, in every child table, inside the transaction that changed one value — and by the HOT rule above, each of those rewrites forfeits HOT and touches every index on the child table.

The shape of the failure

One user correcting a typo in their email address becomes an unbounded write amplification: every order, every order line, every audit row that referenced that address, rewritten in one transaction, each losing HOT and updating every index it sits in. Lesson 10 is about why anyone would have built that schema in the first place.

Check yourself

Updating one indexed column on a row forfeits HOT. What else does it cost?

Losing HOT means a genuinely new heap tuple, and every index on the table must now point at it — not only the index containing the column you changed. That is why the condition is worded as “any columns referenced by the table's indexes”: HOT is a property of the whole row, not of one index.

TOAST is a non-issue

bigint and uuid are fixed-length and not TOAST-able at all. A 26- or 36-character text identifier is TOAST-able in principle but sits two orders of magnitude below the trigger. TOAST never enters this decision — mentioned here only so that when you meet it in an argument you can dismiss it with a number.

Two cautions on measuring

Access is restricted. pgstattuple defaults to the pg_stat_scan_tables role, and of pageinspect the manual says “All of these functions may be used only by superusers.”

Two of the headline numbers are undefined. pgstatindex reports avg_leaf_density — “Average density of leaf pages” — and leaf_fragmentation — “Leaf page fragmentation”. Those one-line descriptions are the complete documentation. The manual does not say how either is computed, gives no units for leaf_fragmentation, and names no threshold for “bad”. Anyone quoting a target number for these is not quoting PostgreSQL.

Check yourself

Someone tells you your index is unhealthy because leaf_fragmentation is above 20. What is wrong with that?

“Leaf page fragmentation” is the entire documented definition — no computation, no units, no threshold. So 20 cannot be compared against anything PostgreSQL says, and any target figure for it came from somewhere else. pgstatindex is in pgstattuple, which defaults to the pg_stat_scan_tables role rather than requiring superuser; it is pageinspect that is superuser-only.

Where this is going

The mechanics are done. You can now price a key's width, its insert pattern, and its mutability, and you know which of those claims are documented and which are inferred.

Read next, from the source

PostgreSQL 18 manual, §66.7 “Heap-Only Tuples (HOT)” — one screen long, and it contains the strongest argument in this whole topic. Read the two conditions slowly and notice that the first one says “the table's indexes”, plural. That plural is the entire case against mutable keys.

Your agent is your teacher. Worth asking about: whether bottom-up index deletion weakens the immutability argument. It genuinely does soften update churn — the manual's claim is strong — but it is aimed at version churn from ordinary UPDATEs, not at a cascading key change, and that distinction is worth working through rather than taking on trust.
