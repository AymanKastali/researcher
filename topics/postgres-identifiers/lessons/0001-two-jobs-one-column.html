Lesson 0001

Two jobs, one column

The mission is to choose from evidence rather than folklore. This lesson establishes what is actually being chosen — which turns out not to be a data type, and turns out not to be settled by the relational model either.

Most arguments about primary keys go badly because the participants are optimising different things without saying so. Before any candidate is named, it is worth being precise about what a key column is being asked to do, and about how little authority the usual appeals to authority actually carry.

The two jobs

A key column is asked to do two things that have almost nothing in common:

Two jobs, judged on disjoint criteria.
Job What it involves Judged on
Internal identity Join children to parents, anchor indexes, survive physical reorganisation of the table. Width, ordering, immutability.
External reference Appear in URLs, API payloads, support tickets, webhook bodies — anywhere outside your database. Opacity, portability, and whether a client can mint one before the database has seen the row.

A single column can do both, and for most systems it should. But notice that no criterion appears in both rows. Nothing that makes a key good at joining makes it good at appearing in a URL, and nothing that makes it good in a URL makes it good at joining.

The framing this topic rests on

The moment the two sets of requirements conflict, you are no longer choosing a key type. You are choosing which job to sacrifice. Every lesson from here is either about measuring one of those sacrifices, or — in lesson 13 — about refusing to make it.

What the relational model actually says

The term comes from Codd's 1970 paper, and it is worth reading what he wrote rather than what is usually attributed to him.

primary key

Normally, one domain (or combination of domains) of a given relation has values which uniquely identify each element (n-tuple) of that relation. Such a domain (or combination) is called a primary key. In the example above, part number would be a primary key, while part colour would not be. A primary key is nonredundant if it is either a simple domain (not a combination) or a combination such that none of the participating simple domains is superfluous in uniquely identifying each element. A relation may possess more than one nonredundant primary key. […] Whenever a relation has two or more nonredundant primary keys, one of them is arbitrarily selected and called the primary key of that relation.

E. F. Codd, “A Relational Model of Data for Large Shared Data Banks”, Communications of the ACM 13(6), June 1970, p. 379. Quoted from the University of Pennsylvania scan of the original; canonical record at dl.acm.org (paywalled).

Two things in that passage are routinely misremembered.

First, the choice among candidates is arbitrary. Codd attaches no significance whatever to which one you pick. That single clause disarms every appeal to “the relational model” made in defence of a particular key style — the model has no opinion, and says so.

Second, Codd's own worked example is a natural key. part number is data that means something to someone outside the database. Codd is not the authority for surrogate keys; the word “surrogate” does not appear in the 1970 paper at all.

A citation to stop making

“Codd said to use surrogate keys” is false. He did not use the term, and his example is a natural key. If you want an authority for surrogate keys you will have to find a different one — and a good part of this topic is discovering how few load-bearing claims in this area have an authority behind them at all.

His definition of a foreign key, from the same page, is the one that makes lesson 13 coherent:

Check yourself

In Codd's 1970 paper, how is the primary key chosen when a relation has several nonredundant candidates?

“Arbitrarily selected” are Codd's own words. The paper attaches no significance to which candidate becomes the primary key, which is exactly why the relational model cannot be cited in support of any particular key style. The other three answers all describe criteria that came later, from practice rather than from the model.

What PostgreSQL enforces

Now the engine, whose position is narrower and more interesting than the theory's.

The parenthesis is the sentence to carry forward. A UNIQUE constraint plus NOT NULL is “functionally almost the same thing” as a primary key. Both produce the same unique B-tree index. In PostgreSQL, the primary key is a designation, not a distinct physical structure.

The one privilege the designation carries is documented on the same page: “the primary key defines the default target column(s) for foreign keys referencing its table.” That is a convenience about what REFERENCES orders means when you do not name a column — not a performance property.

Why this matters more than it sounds

Almost every sentence of the form “the primary key is faster” is really a claim about a B-tree index, and becomes checkable the moment it is restated that way. Lessons 2 to 5 are entirely about that index, and none of them will need the phrase “primary key” to make their argument.

Check yourself

In PostgreSQL, what does the PRIMARY KEY designation give you that UNIQUE NOT NULL does not?

Being the default foreign-key target is the only privilege the manual attaches to the designation. Both constructions build the same unique B-tree index, so there is no density or speed difference. Physical row order is not a PostgreSQL concept at all — lesson 4 is about why. Immutability comes from GENERATED ALWAYS AS IDENTITY, not from PRIMARY KEY, and is lesson 7.

Where this is going

You now know what the decision is — a trade between two jobs — and that neither Codd nor PostgreSQL will make it for you. What you do not yet have is any way to price either job.

That pricing is physical, and it happens inside a B-tree leaf page. Lesson 2 opens one up and counts the bytes — including a demonstration that the arithmetic is right, which matters more than usual here, because most of the numbers this subject circulates are not in any specification.

Read next, from the source

E. F. Codd, “A Relational Model of Data for Large Shared Data Banks” (1970) — read pages 377–380, which is where the definitions live. It is a scanned PDF of a 1970 journal and it reads nothing like a modern database text, which is the point: you will remember that the “arbitrarily selected” clause is really there, because you will have seen it in the original typesetting.

Your agent is your teacher. If the two-jobs framing feels like an artificial split — “it's just an id, it does one thing” — say so. That objection is reasonable and it is worth taking apart now, because lesson 13 exists entirely to resolve it.

On spacing

The goal is retention in a year, not comprehension today, and retention is built by recalling things once you have started to forget them. Leave a day before lesson 2 if you can, and before you open it, try to state the two jobs and their criteria out loud from memory.
