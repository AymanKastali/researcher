Lesson 06 · Transactional Outbox

Operating It

The design is finished. Now something has to watch it, something has to give up on a message that will never succeed, and something has to stop the table growing forever — and the first two are in direct conflict with the ordering you just bought.

The win for this lesson: by the end you can answer "what would you put on the dashboard, and what happens to a message that can never be processed?" You will name the one metric that distinguishes busy from stuck, price the poison-message trade against the ordering guarantee from Lesson 05, and give a retention plan for all three tables you now own.

This closes the mission's production-failure list. Ordering and multiple relay instances are done; poison messages, relay lag and outbox bloat are what is left, and they turn out to be one problem wearing three coats.

Count lies. Age does not.

The instinct is to graph the backlog: count(*) WHERE published_at IS NULL. It is the wrong metric, and it is wrong in the direction that hurts. Forty thousand unpublished rows draining at ten thousand a second is a healthy system under load. One unpublished row that has been sitting there for nine minutes is an outage. Count cannot tell those apart.

The metric that can is the age of the oldest unpublished row. It is the end-to-end latency of the relay expressed as a single number, and it is bounded by design: in a healthy system it never exceeds one poll interval plus one publish.

SELECT abs(hashtext(aggregate_id::text)) % :shard_count AS shard,
       count(*)                             AS backlog,
       max(now() - created_at)              AS oldest,
       max(attempts)                        AS worst_attempts
FROM outbox_message
WHERE published_at IS NULL
GROUP BY 1;

The GROUP BY is not decoration. Lesson 05 gave each shard its own worker, so one dead worker means one stalled shard — and a global max() over eight shards reports the stall while a global avg() hides it entirely. Measured cost of this query against a 106 MB outbox with a 500-row backlog: 13 buffers, an index scan over the backlog only.

Three alerts fall straight out of it, and each one names a different failure:

What firesWhat it means, and what you do
Oldest row exceeds a small multiple of your poll interval, on any single shard That shard's worker is dead, wedged, or blocked behind something. Because it is per-shard, the alert names the worker. Derive the threshold from your own poll interval rather than copying a number — healthy lag is the poll interval.
Backlog growing monotonically while lag also grows Publishing is slower than writing. This is a capacity problem, not a fault: more shards, more workers, or a bigger batch. It is the only one of the three that is fixed by scaling.
Any row with attempts above zero and climbing A poison message, before it has become an incident. This is the cheapest alert in the system and the one people forget to write, because Lesson 02 added the column and then nothing ever read it.
Why age is the honest metric

Every other number describes the relay. Age describes the promise — how stale the world's view of your data is right now. That is the number a downstream team actually cares about, and it is the one you can put in an SLO.

It also degrades gracefully into the answer for the next question. "What is your event latency?" is the same query.

A poison message in the relay stops everything behind it

The claim query orders by id. If the row at the head can never be published — a payload the broker rejects, an aggregate the serialiser chokes on, a routing key bound to nothing — then every poll returns that same row, forever.

-- poll 1                          -- poll 2, and every poll after it
 id | event_type | attempts        id | event_type | attempts
----+------------+----------      ----+------------+----------
  1 | paid       |       12         1 | paid       |       12
  2 | shipped    |        0         2 | shipped    |        0

Measured, PostgreSQL 17.11. The head never moves, because nothing was published and published_at is what moves it. SKIP LOCKED does not save you here: it skips rows another transaction holds right now, and between attempts nobody holds this one.

So exclude it. Give the outbox the delivery limit that the broker gives the queue:

SELECT * FROM outbox_message
WHERE published_at IS NULL
  AND attempts < :max_attempts     -- looks like the obvious fix
ORDER BY id
LIMIT 100
FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED;
Verified: that fix undoes Lesson 05

Two aggregates, one with a stuck first event. With attempts < 10 in the claim query, here is what the relay would publish:

   agg    |  would_publish
----------+-----------------
 11111111 | shipped
 22222222 | paid -> shipped

An order announced as shipped that was never announced as paid — precisely the failure Lesson 05 opened with, reintroduced by the poison-message fix. Skipping a row does not skip an event; it skips a state transition, and everything after it is now a lie.

The unit you must skip is not the row. It is the aggregate. Park the whole stream and let the rest of the shard drain around it:

SELECT * FROM outbox_message o
WHERE o.published_at IS NULL
  AND o.attempts < :max_attempts
  AND NOT EXISTS (                       -- nothing overtakes a parked aggregate
        SELECT 1 FROM outbox_message p
        WHERE p.aggregate_id = o.aggregate_id
          AND p.published_at IS NULL
          AND p.attempts >= :max_attempts)
ORDER BY o.id
LIMIT 100
FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED;

Same fixture, this query: aggregate 22222222 drains both its events, aggregate 11111111 publishes nothing at all. The guarantee holds — a consumer that never hears about order 42 again is recoverable; a consumer told it shipped without being paid is not.

Two things to say before you are asked. First, the correlated subquery runs on every candidate row — the same evaluation-order lesson that killed the advisory lock in Lesson 05 — so at real volume you keep a small parked_aggregate table written when a row hits the limit, and anti-join that instead. Second, a parked aggregate is a human event, not a retry: the row needs last_error read by a person, and unparking is a deliberate act. That is a feature. Nothing in this design silently discards a business fact.

Here is the version you would ship, rather than a description of it:

CREATE TABLE parked_aggregate (
    aggregate_type text        NOT NULL,
    aggregate_id   text        NOT NULL,
    parked_at      timestamptz NOT NULL DEFAULT now(),
    reason         text        NOT NULL,
    PRIMARY KEY (aggregate_type, aggregate_id)
);

SELECT * FROM outbox_message o
WHERE o.published_at IS NULL
  AND NOT EXISTS (                       -- one small table, not a self-scan
        SELECT 1 FROM parked_aggregate p
        WHERE p.aggregate_type = o.aggregate_type
          AND p.aggregate_id   = o.aggregate_id)
ORDER BY o.id
LIMIT 100
FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED;

The self-referencing version scans the outbox twice; this one probes a table that holds one row per parked aggregate — normally zero rows, and a table you can read at a glance during an incident.

Record the failure in its own committed write — UPDATE … SET attempts = attempts + 1, last_error = … — before the transaction that failed to publish ends. If you let the error handling roll back with the work, the attempt count never rises and the row retries forever at full speed, which is the same outage plus a hot loop.

A poison message in the consumer: let the broker count — if you let it

The consumer side already has this built, and it is one of the reasons Lesson 03 chose quorum queues. RabbitMQ counts the failures for you: "Quorum queues keep track of the number of unsuccessful (re)delivery attempts and expose it in the x-delivery-count header that is included with any redelivered message." And it acts on the count — "When a message has been redelivered more times than the limit the message will be dropped (removed) or dead-lettered (if a DLX is configured)."

queue = await channel.declare_queue(
    "order.3",
    durable=True,
    arguments={
        "x-queue-type": "quorum",
        "x-single-active-consumer": True,      # Lesson 05, unchanged
        "x-dead-letter-exchange": "outbox.dlx",
        "x-dead-letter-routing-key": "order.parked",
    },
)

Shown as queue arguments so the whole declaration reads in one place. The docs prefer policies for exactly this: "Hardcoded x-arguments are strongly recommended against since they cannot be updated without redeploying applications and deleting the queue before it can be redeclared, while policies can be updated at any moment." Set delivery-limit and dead-letter-exchange by policy in production.

You get the limit whether you ask for it or not — "Starting with RabbitMQ 4.0, the delivery limit for quorum queues defaults to 20" — and turning it off is explicitly discouraged: "The value of -1 disables the limit altogether. It is not recommended that users disable this limit as repeated requeues can threaten the stability of a queue or RabbitMQ cluster."

The limit does not count the rejection you are most likely to write

"Let the broker count" is only true if you return the message the way the broker counts. It does not count all of them. From the same page: "Unlimited explicit returns (via nack or AMQP 1.0 modify with delivery_failed=false) are allowed without counting toward the delivery limit." The counter moves for "reject, AMQP 1.0 modify with delivery_failed=true, or channel termination with pending messages."

Measured, because this one is worth checking. A quorum queue with x-delivery-limit: 3 and a DLX bound. A consumer that catches its exception and calls message.nack(requeue=True) got the same poison message 12 times and was still going when the run was stopped — never counted, never dead-lettered. The same queue, same limit, with the message returned by closing the channel instead: 4 deliveries, then gone. The limit works. It just never sees a polite nack.

So the retry loop you write by reflex — except Exception: await message.nack(requeue=True) — is the one shape the delivery limit will not end. Use reject when you mean "count this against the message", and keep nack for "put it back, this was my fault, not the message's".

Your dead-letter path can lose the message

Two sentences from the same page, and both belong in your answer. "Dead-lettered messages are re-published without publisher confirms turned on internally. Therefore using DLX in a clustered RabbitMQ environment is not guaranteed to be safe. Messages can be lost if the target queue is not available to accept messages."

The default strategy is the unsafe one — "at-most-once remains the default dead-letter-strategy for quorum queues" — and the fix is to ask for the other: "Quorum queues support a safer form of dead-lettering that uses at-least-once guarantees for the message transfer between queues."

Noticing that the graveyard has a hole in it is the kind of detail that ends the question.

The dead-lettered copy carries its own history: an x-death header whose array is "ordered by recency, that is the most recent dead-lettering event is recorded in the first array element", with the queue, reason and count. That is your triage data — read it before you replay anything.

Dead-lettering can loop, and RabbitMQ guards against it: "It is possible to form a cycle of message dead-lettering where the same message reaches the same queue twice. To prevent automatic infinite message looping within RabbitMQ, RabbitMQ will detect a cycle and drop the message if there was no rejection in the entire cycle." Note the escape clause — a cycle that does contain a rejection is not caught, so a requeue-on-error handler wired back to its own queue is still yours to get wrong.

The trade you are actually making

Ordering and poison-message isolation pull in opposite directions, and there is no position that gets both. You bought per-aggregate order by making each stream serial, and serial is exactly what turns one bad message into a stalled stream. Every mitigation — the delivery limit, the dead-letter exchange, the parked aggregate — is a decision to break that order at a stated point.

So state the point. "After twenty attempts we stop, dead-letter it, and page someone" is an answer. "It retries until it works" is an outage with a countdown on it.

Bloat: the failure nothing warns you about

The outbox is close to the worst-case shape for PostgreSQL's storage model. Every row is inserted, updated exactly once when the relay marks it published, and then deleted. Both the update and the delete leave a dead tuple behind, because "an UPDATE or DELETE of a row does not immediately remove the old version of the row."

Here is what that costs, measured on 200,000 rows:

AfterTotal relation size
Inserting 200,000 events61 MB — 52 MB of heap
The relay setting published_at on each one 119 MB — 106 MB of heap
Deleting 91% of them, then a plain VACUUM 119 MB, holding 17,996 live rows
VACUUM FULL24 kB, and an ACCESS EXCLUSIVE lock for the duration

PostgreSQL 17.11, autovacuum disabled on the table so the numbers are of a known vacuum. Two things to take away: one UPDATE per row doubled the heap, and deleting nine rows in ten returned nothing to the operating system.

The second result is the documented behaviour, not a surprise: plain VACUUM "will not return the space to the operating system, except in the special case where one or more pages at the end of a table become entirely free and an exclusive table lock can be easily obtained." A retention job deletes the oldest rows, so the pages it frees are at the front of the heap. The exception never applies.

Verified: the relay never notices, and that is the danger

On that same 106 MB table holding 18,000 live rows, the claim query touched 104 buffers and the per-shard lag query touched 13. Both read only the backlog through the partial index from Lesson 02.

So there is no latency signal, no slow query, no alert. The partial index that makes the relay fast is precisely what makes bloat invisible — the system stays healthy on every metric you are watching, right up until the disk fills. Monitor pg_total_relation_size on the outbox as a first-class metric, because nothing else will tell you.

Retention: delete rows, or drop tables

The batched delete is the obvious approach and it works. Keep the window generous, keep the batches small enough not to hold a long transaction, and let autovacuum do the rest.

DELETE FROM outbox_message
WHERE id IN (SELECT id FROM outbox_message
             WHERE published_at < now() - interval '7 days'
             ORDER BY id LIMIT 10000);

Batched by id, not by a bare WHERE on the whole window — a single delete of a week's traffic is one long transaction, one lock set, and one enormous burst of dead tuples for autovacuum to chase.

The alternative removes the work rather than scheduling it. Range-partition the outbox by day, and retention becomes a DROP TABLE. The docs put the case plainly: "Dropping an individual partition using DROP TABLE, or doing ALTER TABLE DETACH PARTITION, is far faster than a bulk operation. These commands also entirely avoid the VACUUM overhead caused by a bulk DELETE."

CREATE TABLE outbox_message (
    id           bigint GENERATED ALWAYS AS IDENTITY,
    -- … all of Lesson 02's columns, unchanged …
    created_at   timestamptz NOT NULL DEFAULT now(),
    published_at timestamptz,
    PRIMARY KEY (created_at, id)         -- the partition key must be in the key
) PARTITION BY RANGE (created_at);

CREATE TABLE outbox_message_2026_09_10 PARTITION OF outbox_message
    FOR VALUES FROM ('2026-09-10') TO ('2026-09-11');

CREATE INDEX outbox_unpublished_idx ON outbox_message (id) WHERE published_at IS NULL;

The partial index is declared once on the parent and Postgres creates a local partial index on every partition. Verified: both children reported indpred IS NOT NULL.

Nothing creates tomorrow's partition for you

The obvious follow-up question — who makes the next partition? — has a blunt answer in the PostgreSQL docs, and it is not "Postgres does". There is no built-in scheduler. The manual simply tells you to build one: "In the above example we would be creating a new partition each month, so it might be wise to write a script that generates the required DDL automatically."

For an outbox that is sharper than it sounds, because of what happens when the script does not run: "Inserting data into the parent table that does not map to one of the existing partitions will cause an error; an appropriate partition must be added manually." The outbox insert shares a transaction with the business write — that is the whole pattern — so a missing partition does not fail the relay or delay an event. It fails the order. Partitioning moves a retention problem into the checkout path.

Two things make that safe, and say both: create partitions well ahead (a scheduled job building the next N days, monitored like any other job), and attach a DEFAULT partition so an unmapped row lands somewhere instead of raising. The default partition is a shock absorber, not a plan — rows in it still have to be moved before that range can be attached.

Removing 200,000 published rowsCost
DELETE, then the VACUUM it makes necessary 122 ms + 29 ms, and the space is not returned
DROP TABLE on the day's partition 9 ms, no vacuum, 60 MB back immediately

Do not quote the ratio — it is a small fixture. Quote the shapes: DELETE is linear in rows and manufactures dead tuples, DROP is an unlink and manufactures none. The gap widens with every row you add.

Measured: partitioning costs the claim query nothing

The obvious objection is that ORDER BY id now has to merge across partitions. It does — the plan gains a Merge Append — and on identical data (200,000 rows, 500 unpublished) the buffer counts were 203 partitioned versus 202 flat. Each partition's partial index is scanned in id order and the results are merged; nothing is re-sorted.

The real cost is elsewhere, and the docs name it: "Too many partitions can mean longer query planning times and higher memory consumption during both query planning and execution." Daily partitions with a seven-day window is seven tables. Hourly partitions with a year's retention is a different conversation.

Three tables, and only two of them get pruned

TableRetention
Outbox — grows with event volume Partition by day, drop past your window. Keep it long enough to replay from, which means long enough to survive a weekend.
Inbox — grows with event volume Delete rows older than your maximum plausible redelivery delay. The delivery limit now bounds that: once a message dead-letters it is never redelivered, so the window you must cover is the retry window, not eternity. There is still no number in any doc — derive yours from your own limit and backoff.
Aggregate cursor — grows with your domain Do not prune it on age. One row per aggregate, not per event, and deleting a row re-opens the gate a stale event would have been stopped by. Prune it only when the aggregate itself is deleted, in the same transaction.

The asymmetry is the point, and it is worth saying out loud: two of these tables are logs and one is state. Logs age out. State does not.

The runbook, in one page

SymptomCause, and first move
One shard's oldest row is climbing, others flat That worker is down or wedged. Restart it; SKIP LOCKED and the shard filter make that safe mid-flight.
Every shard's oldest row is climbing together The broker or the database, not the relay. Check publisher confirms failing and check the outbox is writable.
Lag flat and low, backlog growing Not a fault. You are write-bound; add shards and workers, and remember the reshard needs a drain.
A row's attempts is climbing Poison message. Read last_error now, before the limit parks the whole aggregate.
Messages appearing in the dead-letter queue Read x-death for the reason and count. Fix, then republish deliberately — the inbox makes the replay safe.
Disk filling, every latency metric normal Bloat. Nothing else presents this way. Check pg_total_relation_size against live row count, and check the retention job is actually running.
What you can now do

Name age-of-oldest-unpublished as the health metric and say why count is not; write the per-shard lag query; explain why a poison row stalls a shard and why the obvious fix reintroduces the ordering bug; park an aggregate instead of skipping a row; configure the quorum-queue delivery limit and a DLX, and volunteer that the default dead-letter strategy is at-most-once and can lose the message; and give a retention plan that distinguishes the two tables that age out from the one that must not.

Name what is still broken

  • Replay is described, not designed. "Republish from the dead-letter queue" needs a tool, and that tool needs to decide whether it re-enters at the original routing key or a repair queue.
  • The dead-letter hop did not do what the docs say, and that is unresolved. In the run above the counted-out message left the queue after four deliveries — but it never appeared on the bound dead-letter queue, which was empty. The docs say "dropped (removed) or dead-lettered (if a DLX is configured)", and a DLX was configured. Something about that setup is wrong, or the at-most-once default dropped it silently. Until it is chased down, treat the x-death field list here as quoted, not observed, and do not promise an interviewer that a counted-out message is definitely retrievable.
  • No number for retention, still. "Seven days" is an illustration. The delivery limit bounds the redelivery window in principle, but only for returns the broker counts — which, as above, is not the return most consumers send.

Check yourself

One attempt per question. Two of these reach back to earlier lessons on purpose.

  1. Which single metric tells you whether the relay is healthy?

    Count cannot separate busy from stuck. Forty thousand rows draining fast is healthy; one row stuck for nine minutes is an outage, and count ranks them the wrong way round.

    Correct. It is the relay's end-to-end latency as one number, bounded in a healthy system by one poll interval plus one publish — and it is the number a downstream team actually cares about.

    Throughput describes the relay, not the promise. A high publish rate is perfectly compatible with one aggregate stalled since Tuesday.

    Worth watching, but it is the consumer's health. A relay that has stopped publishing shows an empty queue, which looks like calm.

  2. A row at the head of a shard fails on every publish attempt, and the claim query is Lesson 02's unchanged. What happens to that shard?

    SKIP LOCKED skips rows another transaction is holding at that instant. Between attempts nobody holds this row, so every poll picks it up again.

    That is what the naive attempts < N filter buys you — and this question is about the query without it. Unfiltered, ORDER BY id means the head has to move before anything else can.

    Correct, and measured: the same two rows come back on every poll. published_at is what advances the head, and a row that never publishes never sets it.

    The delivery limit is a queue feature. This message never reached the broker — the outbox has no limit unless you build one.

  3. Why is adding AND attempts < 10 to the claim query the wrong way to get past a poison row?

    The index is still chosen — the predicate that selects it is published_at IS NULL, and an extra filter on a cheap column does not displace it.

    Where you increment it is a real design question, but it is not the flaw here. The filter is wrong even with a perfectly accurate count.

    They are still there and still deletable. Retention filters on published_at, which is unrelated to the attempt count.

    Correct, and measured: the relay publishes shipped for an aggregate whose paid it skipped. That is Lesson 05's opening failure, reintroduced. Park the aggregate, not the row.

  4. A message in a shard queue can never be handled successfully. What ends the retry loop?

    Correct — with the condition that decides whether it is true in your code. Quorum queues count failed deliveries and, past the limit, drop or dead-letter; configure a DLX so it is the second one. But the count only moves for reject or a dropped channel. A consumer that returns the message with nack(requeue=True) is never counted out — measured at 12 deliveries and still cycling against a limit of 3.

    Prefetch bounds how many deliveries are in flight. It never terminates anything — a single message failing forever satisfies any prefetch setting.

    Confirms are the publish half, and this message was published successfully. Nothing about the consumer's failure travels back to them.

    The inbox row is written in the same transaction as the work, so a failure rolls it back too. That is deliberate — otherwise a crash mid-work would mark the message done.

  5. Reaching back to Lesson 02. The outbox holds 18,000 live rows in 106 MB and no alert has fired. Why not?

    Autovacuum's thresholds decide when it runs, not what you are told. It does not raise alerts at all.

    Correct, and this is the trap. The claim query touched 104 buffers on that table because the partial index only indexes the backlog. No latency signal, no slow query — the metric you must add is table size itself.

    The opposite: dead tuples occupy pages and are counted in full. That 106 MB is almost entirely them.

    It fetches the payload, so it does read the heap — just only for the backlog rows. It is the index's narrowness that hides the bloat, not an index-only scan.

  6. Reaching back to Lesson 05. Which of your three tables must not be pruned on a time window?

    Retention filters on published_at, so unpublished rows are never candidates. The outbox is pruned, just not blindly.

    It is pruned on a window — you just have to choose one longer than your retry window, which the delivery limit now bounds.

    Correct. It is one row per aggregate, not per event, so it grows with your domain rather than your traffic. Delete a cursor row and the next stale event for that aggregate walks straight through the gate.

    Two of them are logs and one is state. That asymmetry is the answer — the cursor is not a record of what happened, it is the thing that decides what happens next.

Say it from memory

Close the page. Ninety seconds, out loud: "you've built the outbox — what do you monitor, and what happens to a message that can never be processed?"

One metric above all others: the age of the oldest unpublished row, grouped by shard. Count can't distinguish busy from stuck — forty thousand rows draining fast is healthy and one row stuck for nine minutes is an outage. Age is the relay's end-to-end latency as a single number, and in a healthy system it never exceeds a poll interval plus a publish. Per shard, because one worker owns each shard, so a global average hides a dead one. Then two more: backlog growing while lag grows means I'm write-bound and should add shards, and any row whose attempt count is climbing is a poison message before it's an incident.

For a message that can never be processed, there are two sides. On the consumer side the broker already counts for me: quorum queues track failed deliveries in x-delivery-count and past the delivery limit — 20 by default in RabbitMQ 4 — they drop or dead-letter it. So I configure a dead-letter exchange and read the x-death header for triage. I'd flag that the default dead-letter strategy is at-most-once and the docs say DLX in a cluster isn't guaranteed safe, so I'd turn on at-least-once dead-lettering.

On the relay side nothing counts for me, so I build it. A row at the head that never publishes stops the whole shard, because ORDER BY id means the head has to move and SKIP LOCKED doesn't help — nobody's holding it between attempts. The obvious fix, filtering on attempts, is wrong: it publishes an aggregate's later events after skipping an earlier one, which is exactly the ordering bug the previous lesson eliminated. So I park the whole aggregate, let the rest of the shard drain around it, and treat unparking as a human decision.

And the one nothing warns you about is bloat. Every outbox row is inserted, updated once when it's published, then deleted — the update alone doubles the heap, and a plain vacuum after a retention delete doesn't return space to the OS because the freed pages are at the front of the table. The relay never notices, because the partial index means its cost tracks the backlog and not the table, so every latency metric stays green until the disk fills. I'd monitor table size directly and partition by day so retention is a DROP TABLE rather than a delete plus a vacuum.

Three tables, and only two of them age out: the outbox and the inbox are logs, but the aggregate cursor is state — one row per aggregate — and deleting it re-opens the gate for a stale event.

Scoring: the three costly omissions are reaching for count instead of age, skipping the row rather than parking the aggregate, and forgetting that bloat is silent. Bonus for volunteering that the dead-letter path can itself lose the message.
