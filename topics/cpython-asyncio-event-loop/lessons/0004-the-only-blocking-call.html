Lesson 0004

The only blocking call

Steps 2 and 3. How long the loop is prepared to sleep, why the answer is one of exactly three things, and what the kernel is actually asked for once the decision is made.

There is exactly one point in the whole of _run_once where the process voluntarily waits. Everything you know about asyncio's operational behaviour — “don't block the loop”, “use an executor for CPU work” — is a statement about the fact that this point exists and is reached only once per iteration.

The three timeouts, and what each one means.
Condition timeout Effect
_ready is non-empty, or stop() has been called 0 The selector is still consulted, but returns immediately. There is work to do, or the loop is on its way out.
Nothing ready, but timers are scheduled head._when - now, clamped to [0, 86400] Sleep until the next deadline, or until an fd becomes ready — whichever comes first.
Nothing ready, nothing scheduled None Block indefinitely. The only things that can wake the process are a monitored fd and the self-pipe.

The first case is the reason the documented stop() behaviour is what it is: calling stop() before run_forever() means _stopping is already true when the first iteration computes its timeout, so the loop polls once with zero and then exits. PEP 3156 expected something different, and lesson 15 puts the two texts side by side.

Both clamps earn their place

Below, at zero. A timer already past due would give a negative timeout, and some selectors read a negative value as “block forever” — which is the worst possible response to “this was due a second ago”.

Above, at MAXIMUM_SELECT_TIMEOUT, which is 24 * 3600 with the comment “Maximum timeout passed to select to avoid OS limitations” (base_events.py:67-68). This is not a limit on how long a timer can be. A timer thirty days out simply costs thirty iterations of _run_once, each waking, finding the head still in the future, and re-blocking for another day. Two details here matter later.

None becomes -1, which is epoll_wait's “block forever”. A loop with nothing ready and no timers is genuinely parked in the kernel with no deadline at all. The only thing that will bring it back is a file descriptor — which is why call_soon_threadsafe has to write a byte to one.

Sub-millisecond timeouts round away from zero. A 1.2 ms asyncio timeout becomes a 2 ms epoll_wait, deliberately, so the sleep is never short. Hold that thought: lesson 11 shows step 5 rounding in the opposite direction, and the two roughly cancel.

The contract the loop is relying on

What “blocking the loop” actually means

Put steps 3 and 6 together and the whole of asyncio's operational folklore falls out of the ordering. The loop reaches step 3 once per iteration. It cannot reach step 3 of iteration N+1 until step 6 of iteration N has returned. So:

The precise statement

A slow callback does not delay itself. It delays the poll. For as long as it runs, no file descriptor in the process is examined at all — not read, not written, not even checked. Your sockets are not slow; they are invisible.

The documentation states the consequence and leaves the mechanism implicit: “Blocking (CPU-bound) code should not be called directly. For example, if a function performs a CPU-intensive calculation for 1 second, all concurrent asyncio Tasks and IO operations would be delayed by 1 second” (Running Blocking Code, Doc/library/asyncio-dev.rst:122-125). [guaranteed]

This is also why the debug-mode slow-callback log from lesson 3 is worth more than it looks. It is not measuring how long your function took; it is measuring how long the process was blind.

Check yourself

The loop has an empty _ready, one timer due in 40 hours, and two registered sockets. What timeout does step 2 compute?

MAXIMUM_SELECT_TIMEOUT caps any single poll at one day, so a 40-hour timer costs two iterations: one that sleeps a day and finds the head still in the future, and one that sleeps the remaining 16 hours. Registered sockets do not shorten the timeout — being registered is not being ready.

Check yourself

Why does EpollSelector round a sub-millisecond timeout upward rather than to nearest?

The source comment says it directly: epoll_wait() has millisecond resolution, so rounding away from zero waits at least the requested time. Waking early would mean an extra pointless iteration; waking late by under a millisecond is harmless. Step 5 of _run_once deliberately errs the other way, which is lesson 11.

Where this is going

The poll has returned with a list of ready descriptors. Lesson 5 is the twelve lines that turn that list into _ready entries — and the architectural seam those twelve lines sit on.

The three timeouts, and the two clamps applied to them, are tabulated in the _run_once reference. That is the version to consult later; this one is the version that explains why each value is what it is.
