CPython's asyncio Event Loop, From the Inside

What BaseEventLoop._run_once() actually does, read out of the source: six steps, two queues, one blocking call, and the three-valued attribute that joins every coroutine you have written to all of it.

Fifteen lessons, built to be spaced over days rather than read in one sitting. The five reference documents at the bottom are the ones designed to be come back to. CPython 3.13.11 is the version taught throughout, and every line citation is against that tag.

Who this is for

Someone who already writes async def and await fluently and wants to know what happens underneath. There is no tutorial content here — no explanation of the syntax, no coroutine primer. Lessons 1 to 6 do not mention await at all.

Lessons

  • 0001 Four lines, two queues

    run_forever is ten lines. stop() is one assignment. The loop's entire scheduling state is a deque and a heapq, and there is no third queue.

  • 0002 Six steps of one iteration

    The map. Polling happens before running, nothing user-written runs outside step 6, and exactly one step can block — which is where “don't block the loop” comes from.

  • 0003 The ntodo snapshot

    The hinge. Four lines that solve fairness and lock-free thread safety at once — plus why a cancelled call_soon handle still consumes a slot.

  • 0004 The only blocking call

    Three timeout cases in priority order, both clamps, and epoll_wait rounding away from zero. The precise statement of what a slow callback costs: not itself, the poll.

  • 0005 Readiness becomes callbacks

    Twelve lines are the whole of asyncio's I/O dispatch, and two NotImplementedError stubs are the whole seam between the scheduler and the multiplexer.

  • 0006 Inside Handle._run

    Context captured at construction, exceptions swallowed into the handler, and the two that are re-raised and can kill a loop. The callback machine is now complete, with no coroutine in it.

  • 0007 The blocking flag

    The second hinge. _asyncio_future_blocking read as a three-valued signal, and why a PEP 3156 clause from 2012 left a duck-typed attribute as the only available tool.

  • 0008 A complete round trip

    One await down to epoll_wait and back — including the fast path where the coroutine never suspends, and the full extra iteration the Future costs.

  • 0009 Who owns what

    Three objects, and the right-hand column of the table does the work: the loop has never heard of await. Plus the Task's own three-state invariant and why cancel() is a request.

  • 0010 The C accelerator

    Task and Future are C types; the loop never can be. Worth 1.1×–1.5×, not 10×, and the reason why is the same PEP clause as lesson 7.

  • 0011 The timer heap

    An ordering that is a preorder rather than a total order, a clock with three different claims attached to it, and a loop that fires timers slightly early on purpose.

  • 0012 Cancelled timers, and sleep

    Step 1: two strategies, two undocumented constants, and a reproducible counter oddity presented as an observation rather than a bug. Plus what sleep(0) actually allocates.

  • 0013 Threads and the self-pipe

    The most operationally useful lesson here. What call_soon from a worker thread really does — a measured 2.8-second stall, unbounded in principle — and the socket that makes the safe version safe.

  • 0014 What changed, 3.11 → 3.13

    Structured concurrency, eager tasks, a free-threading port — and _run_once moved twice, cosmetically. Which tells you what in this topic will survive 3.15.

  • 0015 Where the docs and the source disagree

    Five conflicts, each argued with both texts on the page and each decided — then the deciding turned into four questions to ask of any claim.

How to study this

Storage strength, not fluency

The goal is retention in a year, not comprehension today. That means leaving gaps between lessons, and — before opening the next one — trying to state the previous one's argument out loud from memory. The recall attempt is the learning; re-reading only feels like it.

Lessons 1–6 are the callback machine, 7–10 the coroutine layer, 11–12 timers, 13 threads, 14–15 reading the evidence. The natural long pauses are after 6 and after 10. Lessons 3 and 7 are the two to over-learn: almost everything later leans on one or the other.

On line numbers

Every citation here is exact against tag v3.13.11, because each file was diffed byte-for-byte against the upstream tag before being quoted. That precision is deliberate and it is fragile: a reformat would break every anchor without changing any behaviour. When a new release lands, the version ledger has the checklist.

What is not here

No Windows: ProactorEventLoop was not read, and IOCP is a completion model rather than a readiness one. No third-party loops: uvloop replaces exactly the layer these lessons open up. No streams.py or sslproto.py. And no benchmark presented as a benchmark — the measurements quoted are single-machine and establish orders of magnitude only. Unverified claims records every such limit.
