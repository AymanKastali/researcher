Lesson 0003

The ambiguous failure

A mission success criterion, stated as a sentence you should be able to produce from memory: “either once or not at all, and the user is not told which” — and why no protocol removes it.

You now obey RFC 9110 perfectly. You still have a problem, and it is not a problem about HTTP. It is a property of the channel rather than of the verb, it was described precisely in 1983, and no amount of correct method selection touches it.

1983: the limit of what any request/response protocol can offer

Birrell and Nelson, describing the guarantees of the Cedar RPC system at Xerox PARC, state the boundary plainly. This is the foundational statement of the problem:

Nine words to memorise

“Either once or not at all — the user is not told which.”

This is the ambiguous failure, and it is the reason idempotency keys exist. Everything else in this topic is machinery built in response to this one sentence.

Why the client cannot tell

Maarten van Steen’s companion material for Distributed Systems makes the same point about the client’s epistemic position:

Three utterly different situations, one indistinguishable symptom. The request never arrived; the request arrived and the server died; the request arrived, succeeded, and the reply was lost. From the client’s chair they are the same event: silence.

Transparent recovery is not merely hard — it is impossible

This is the part that usually surprises people, and it is worth sitting with. Consider a server asked to update a document, with three events: M (send the completion message), P (complete the processing), C (crash). Enumerate the orderings:

Now look at cases 2 and 4. In case 2 the update never happened; in case 4 it did. From the client’s side they are indistinguishable from each other and from a simple lost reply. There is no header, no status code and no protocol extension that separates them, because the information the client would need was destroyed along with the server.

Do not look for a protocol fix

A great deal of wasted design effort goes into trying to make the network tell the client what happened. It cannot. The correct response is not a better protocol but a different arrangement: let the operation be attempted more than once, and make the second attempt harmless.

The engineering content of the trilemma is simple and unforgiving. It reduces to a single line of client code — whether or not you retry:

try:
    charge_the_card(amount=2000)
except AmbiguousFailure:

Two real options, and a third that is not a delivery policy at all.
Policy You get You pay
Don’t retry → at-most-once Never double-charge Silently drop work whenever the network hiccups
Retry → at-least-once Never drop work Double-charge whenever a response is lost, not a request
Exactly-once — Not selectable. See below.

“Exactly-once” is not a third option

Kafka’s own documentation is unusually blunt about this, and the honesty is worth quoting in full because it is rare:

The resolution, in every system that achieves the appearance of once, is the same two-part arrangement:

The shape of every real answer

Retry (at-least-once delivery) plus deduplication at the receiver (so that the effect occurs once).

Exactly-once is not something you select. It is something you construct, out of an at-least-once channel and a receiver that remembers.

Kafka does exactly this, and describes the mechanism in terms that should now look extremely familiar:

A producer ID plus a sequence number, kept in a table at the receiver so duplicates can be dropped. That is an idempotency key, under a different name, inside a message broker.

Check yourself

A client sends a charge request and receives no response. What does it know?

Silence is compatible with a lost request, a crashed server, and a lost response — and the client cannot tell which. Each of the other three answers claims knowledge the client provably does not have. If any of them were obtainable, the ambiguous failure would not exist and neither would this topic.

Check yourself

How is exactly-once actually achieved in systems that offer it?

Kafka’s producer ID and sequence number are the receiver-side deduplication half; resending after a network error is the at-least-once half. No transport can help, because the duplicate may originate in the application’s own retry logic rather than in the network — a point the next lesson makes precisely.

The question this leaves open

If the answer is deduplication at the receiver, something has to identify the duplicate. Why must the client supply that identifier? Couldn’t the network layer, or the framework, or the load balancer suppress duplicates on your behalf? There is a firm and famous answer, and it is lesson 4.

Read next, from the source

Birrell & Nelson, Implementing Remote Procedure Calls (Xerox PARC CSL-83-7, 1983) — read §2 and the first few pages of §3. It is the best-written paper in this topic and the most consequential: the guarantee on p. 11 is still the guarantee, forty years on. You will meet its call identifier again in the next lesson, and recognising it as your idempotency key is the moment this subject clicks.

Your agent is your teacher. If the impossibility argument feels like it must have a loophole, push on it — trying to construct the loophole and watching it fail is a far better way to internalise this than accepting it, and the M/P/C orderings give you the tool to test any proposal.

On spacing
