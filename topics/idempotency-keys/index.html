Network Idempotency and Idempotency Keys

Why a network cannot tell a lost request from a lost response, why that forces the client to retry, and why the receiver must therefore deduplicate — from an 1870 algebra text to a lease you should not trust.

Eight lessons, built to be spaced over days rather than read in one sitting. The four reference documents at the bottom are the ones designed to be come back to.

Lessons

  • 0001 What “idempotent” actually means

    Peirce’s 1870 definition, the move to function composition, and the distinction everything else rests on: a function is idempotent by inspection, an effect only by construction.

  • 0002 What HTTP actually promises

    RFC 9110’s two definitions, why safe ⊂ idempotent, why “returns the same response” is wrong, and the buried clause that justifies the Idempotency-Key header.

  • 0003 The ambiguous failure

    “Either once or not at all — the user is not told which.” The 1983 guarantee, the proof that transparent recovery is impossible, and the delivery trilemma with its fake third option.

  • 0004 Why the client generates the key

    The end-to-end argument: TCP deduplicates segments but cannot deduplicate intentions. Plus the header itself, and the generation mistake that silently disables everything.

  • 0005 The lifecycle of a key

    Eleven steps from generation to expiry, the key record as a state machine, the read-path decision table, and the two steps that must be atomic.

  • 0006 The atomic claim

    The time-of-check-to-time-of-use race, and the two good answers — conditional insert and serialisable transaction — in Postgres, DynamoDB and Redis.

  • 0007 Why not a distributed lock

    Kleppmann versus antirez on fencing tokens, where the two actually converge, and the observation that no vendor blocks a concurrent request.

  • 0008 The edges that bite

    Retention as the only failure invisible from both sides, fingerprint traps, scoping as a security requirement, which failures to store, and effectively-once versus exactly-once.

How to study this

Storage strength, not fluency

The goal is retention in a year, not comprehension today. That means leaving gaps between lessons, and — before opening the next one — trying to state the previous one’s argument out loud from memory. The recall attempt is the learning; re-reading only feels like it.

Lessons 1–4 are the “why” and 5–8 are the “how”. The natural long pause is between 4 and 5.
